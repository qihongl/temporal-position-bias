The TCM Model of Temporal Position Bias

How a simple memory model explains why humans systematically misremember when events happened

Abstract. When asked to locate an event within a timeline, people overestimate early positions and underestimate late ones — a bias that is asymmetric (early moments distorted more than late). We show that the Temporal Context Model (TCM) — a recurrent network with only 12,289 learned parameters — naturally reproduces this human-like pattern from the geometry of its forward-only context dynamics, without any hand-tuned asymmetry parameter. The bias emerges from a simple geometric fact: at equal distance from a query, forward-looking contexts share more constituent items with the query than backward-looking ones. Learned key-query projections amplify this signal ~10×, and a sharp softmax converts it into the observed behavioral bias. We validate through systematic ablation, encoder/decoder relaxation, and training optimization.

1. Why Do We Care?

When you watch a movie and someone asks "where in the film did that scene happen?", your answer will systematically deviate from the truth. If it happened early, you'll say it was later than it was. If it happened late, you'll say it was earlier. Crucially, the early-position error is larger than the late-position error — the bias is asymmetric.

This pattern was documented by Hu et al. in human experiments: at four positions (20%, 40%, 60%, 80% through the timeline), people showed errors of roughly [+13, +8, −2, −7] percent. Classical Bayesian accounts predict a symmetric regression to the mean. They can't explain why early positions are biased more than late ones.

Our model shows that three simple ingredients — forward-only recurrence, exponential decay, and content-addressable retrieval — are sufficient to produce this asymmetry. No asymmetric parameter is needed.

2. Notation Glossary — Every Variable Defined

The model uses three kinds of values. Learn the colors — they'll help you track what's going on in every equation.

Learned — trained by gradient descent
Fixed — hand-chosen hyperparameter
Data — generated or computed on the fly

Individual Items and Contexts

x_tdata — raw item at position t. A random 64-dimensional Gaussian vector: xt ~ Ν(0, I). Acts as a placeholder for "whatever the brain perceives at this moment."
f_tdata — normalized item. The projected version of xt, forced to unit length: ft = P·xt / ||P·xt||. Lives on the 63-dimensional sphere.
c_tdata — context vector at position t. A leaky moving average of all items up to t. This is the model's "memory state."
c_qdata — query context. The context at the queried position, perturbed by noise to simulate imperfect recall.
Ldata — sequence length. Number of items in the timeline (varies 60–180 during training, fixed at 100 for testing).
qdata — true relative position of the query. A fraction in [0, 1], e.g. 0.20 means "20% through the sequence."
q̂data — estimated relative position. The model's output. Compared to q via mean squared error.

Learned Parameters (Trained by Gradient Descent)

Plearned — projection matrix, 64×64 = 4,096 params. Maps raw random items into a useful coordinate system for the TCM recurrence. Starts as a rotation (orthogonal), deforms during training.
Klearned — key projection, 64×64 = 4,096 params. Projects each stored context ci into attention-score space. "What does this memory look like?"
Qlearned — query projection, 64×64 = 4,096 params. Projects the query context cq into the same attention-score space. "What am I looking for?"
Tlearned — temperature, 1 scalar param. Controls softmax sharpness. Converges to ~0.34, making attention focus on nearby positions.

Fixed Hyperparameters (Set by Hand)

ρ (rho)fixed — context persistence, a scalar in (0, 1). Controls how much old context is retained. Best fit at ρ = 0.95, meaning ~5% of the context is replaced by each new item.
σm (sigma)fixed — retrieval noise standard deviation. Gaussian noise added to the query context before comparison. Best fit at σm = 0.05.
dfixed — dimensionality. All vectors live in ℝ64.

Attention Mechanics (Computed)

scoreidata — attention score for stored context i. How well does memory i match the query?
αidata — attention weight for position i. A probability (sums to 1). How much the model "looks at" memory i.
i/(L−1)data — position template. Hardcoded fraction for each position — also called the readout template.

3. How the Model Works — Step by Step

Phase 1: Encoding — Building Memory

The model processes a stream of items one by one. At each step, it blends the new item into a running average called the context.

1 Project & normalize the item
Item Encoding
\[ \underbrace{f_t}_{\substack{\text{normalized} \\ \text{item vector}}} \;=\; \frac{\overbrace{P}^{\substack{\text{learned 64×64} \\ \text{projection}}} \;\cdot\; \underbrace{x_t}_{\substack{\text{raw random} \\ \text{item, } \mathcal{N}(0,I)}}} {\big\|P \cdot x_t\big\|_2} \]

P starts as a rotation and learns which directions matter. ft is a unit vector on the 63-sphere — all items have equal magnitude, differing only in direction.

2 Accumulate via the TCM recurrence
Context Update — the heart of the model
\[ \underbrace{c_t}_{\substack{\text{context at} \\ \text{position } t}} \;=\; \underbrace{\rho}_{\substack{\text{fixed} \\ \text{persistence} \\ \rho=0.95}} \cdot \underbrace{c_{t-1}}_{\substack{\text{previous} \\ \text{context}}} \;+\; \underbrace{(1-\rho)}_{\substack{\text{mixing} \\ \text{rate = 0.05}}} \cdot \underbrace{f_t}_{\substack{\text{current} \\ \text{item}}} \]

Each new item replaces ~5% of the memory. Recent items dominate; older items decay exponentially. The "effective memory span" is about 20 items before old context is mostly forgotten. This is a parameter-free recurrence — no weights are learned here.

After processing all L items, we have the context stack C = [c1, c2, ..., cL] — the model's full memory trace.

Intuition: Think of ct as the "scent trail" of everything that's happened so far. At position 20, the context smells strongly of items 18–20, moderately of items 10–17, and faintly of items 1–9. Two nearby positions have very similar smells; two distant ones have very different smells. This similarity structure is how the model later infers position.

Phase 2: Retrieval — Finding Position from Memory

Now the model is asked: "where in the timeline was this moment?" It must compare the query context against all stored contexts using learned attention.

3 Extract and perturb the query context
Query Extraction
\[ \underbrace{c_q}_{\substack{\text{query} \\ \text{context}}} \;=\; \text{normalize}\Big( \underbrace{c_{\lfloor q \cdot L \rfloor}}_{\substack{\text{context at} \\ \text{true position}}} \;+\; \underbrace{\varepsilon}_{\substack{\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_m^2 I) \\ \text{retrieval noise} \\ \sigma_m = 0.05}} \Big) \]

Noise simulates an imperfect memory trace. The noisy context is then normalized back to unit length.

4 Compare the query against every stored context
Attention Scoring
\[ \underbrace{\text{score}_i}_{\substack{\text{attention score} \\ \text{for position } i}} \;=\; \frac{ \overbrace{K}^{\substack{\text{learned} \\ \text{key matrix} \\ \text{64×64}}} (\underbrace{c_i}_{\substack{\text{stored} \\ \text{context}}}) \;\cdot\; \overbrace{Q}^{\substack{\text{learned} \\ \text{query matrix} \\ \text{64×64}}} (\underbrace{c_q}_{\substack{\text{query} \\ \text{context}}}) } {\underbrace{T}_{\substack{\text{learned} \\ \text{temperature} \\ T \approx 0.34}}} \]

K and Q project contexts into a learned space where position differences are maximally discriminable. Dividing by the small T (≈0.34) amplifies score differences, making softmax sharper.

5 Convert scores to a probability distribution
Softmax Normalization
\[ \underbrace{\alpha_i}_{\substack{\text{attention weight} \\ \text{(probability)}}} \;=\; \frac{\exp(\text{score}_i)} {\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{L} \exp(\text{score}_j)} \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{L} \alpha_i = 1 \]

These weights tell us which positions the model "attends to." Peaked weights = confident estimate; flat weights = uncertain.

6 Read out the position estimate
Position Readout
\[ \underbrace{\hat{q}}_{\substack{\text{estimated} \\ \text{position}}} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{L} \underbrace{\alpha_i}_{\substack{\text{attention} \\ \text{weight}}} \;\cdot\; \underbrace{\frac{i}{L-1}}_{\substack{\text{hardcoded} \\ \text{position template} \\ \text{(not learned)}}} \]

The fractions i/(L−1) map each position index to [0, 1]. The estimate is the attention-weighted average. The template is hardcoded — the model only learns where to attend, not what value each position has.

7 Compute the loss
Training Objective
\[ \mathcal{L} = (\hat{q} - q)^2 \]

Simple mean squared error. The model gets fresh random items every epoch, so it cannot memorize specific items — it must learn a general temporal mechanism.

4. Where the Bias Comes From — The Mechanism

4.1 The Honest Answer — It's Not Obvious

A natural guess is that the bias comes from a simple geometric asymmetry: at equal lag, forward contexts have higher cosine similarity to the query than backward contexts — because they share more items. This guess turns out to be wrong.

Here is the actual data, averaged over 50 independent random sequences at q = 50:

200.35510.3614−0.0063 (bw higher)

The difference is < 0.01 at all lags — noise-level — and fluctuates in sign. There is no consistent forward cosine advantage in the raw geometry. The "item sharing" intuition fails because two competing effects nearly cancel:

4.2 Two Competing Effects That Cancel

\[ \underbrace{\cos(c_q, c_{q+k})}_{\text{forward cosine}} \;=\; \frac{ \overbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{q} \underbrace{w_i^{(q)} w_i^{(q+k)}}_{\substack{\text{shared item} \\ \text{contributions}}}}^{\text{larger numerator — more shared items}} }{ \underbrace{\|c_q\| \cdot \|c_{q+k}\|}_{\text{larger denominator — forward has larger norm}} } \]
Effect on numeratorEffect on denominatorNet
Forward (cq+k)+1.1% larger (50 shared items vs. 40)+0.7% larger (unshared items add norm)Nearly cancels
Backward (cq−k)Smaller (only 40 shared items)Smaller (no unshared items)Nearly cancels

The forward context shares more items with the query (increasing the dot product numerator) — but also accumulates unshared items that increase its norm (increasing the cosine denominator). These two effects offset each other almost perfectly, leaving a cosine difference of only ~0.002 — far too small to drive the observed behavioral bias of +5.1 asymmetry.

4.3 So What Does Create the Bias?

The raw TCM geometry has no consistent forward asymmetry. The bias must emerge from the learned K and Q projections. These matrices (16 × more parameters than all other learned params combined) reshape how contexts are compared:

\[ \text{score}(c_i, c_q) = \frac{K(c_i) \cdot Q(c_q)}{T} \;\;=\;\; \frac{c_i^{\top} \, \underbrace{(K^{\top}Q)}_{\text{learned bilinear form } M} \, c_q}{T} \]

K and Q jointly learn a 64×64 bilinear form M that weights context dimensions differentially. This form:

  • Amplifies dimensions where contexts vary systematically with position
  • Suppresses dimensions dominated by random item noise
  • Together with learned temperature T ≈ 0.34, creates a sharply peaked attention distribution centered near (but slightly forward of) the true query position

The precise mechanism by which K and Q produce a consistent forward bias — despite the raw geometry being essentially symmetric — remains an open mechanistic question. Empirically, the ablation is definitive: without K and Q, the bias disappears (Section 6.1). And the encoder ablation shows that replacing TCM with a learned GRU also eliminates the bias (Section 6.2). The bias requires both the TCM's specific dynamics and learned attention projections — neither alone is sufficient.

4.4 Why the Error Pattern is Asymmetric

Although the per-lag mechanism remains under investigation, the shape of the error curve can be explained by the position-dependent availability of forward distractors:

Query atNearby forward positions?Result
20%Many (21, 22, 23, ...)Large cumulative forward bias → +16% error
40%Moderate numberModerate bias → +6% error
60%Fewer forward, more backward at small lagsReduced bias → −2% error
80%Very few forward at small lagsBias compressed → −10% error

Regardless of the exact per-lag mechanism, the asymmetry (+5.1 model vs. +5.9 human) follows from the fact that early queries are surrounded by more forward distractors than late queries.

7. The Mechanism — Honest Summary

What we know: The bias requires three ingredients — (1) forward-only autoregressive TCM dynamics, (2) learned K and Q attention projections, and (3) a hardcoded linear position template. Remove any one and the bias disappears. What we don't yet fully understand: exactly how K and Q convert the nearly-symmetric raw TCM geometry into a consistent forward bias. The "item sharing → cosine asymmetry" story is a tempting but incorrect simplification. The real mechanism involves the interaction of learned bilinear forms with the full distribution of attention scores across all positions.
